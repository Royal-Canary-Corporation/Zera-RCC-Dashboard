Lam Ngọc · Flat Site

Design precedents for a flat site with views to Hạ Long Bay — architecture that creates its own landscape

5 Villas · Flat Terrain · No Existing Topography

♢ New Site Context: Flat Terrain

The Lam Ngọc site is confirmed flat — no existing cliffs, hills, or rock formations to build around. The primary natural asset is the view to Hạ Long Bay's limestone karsts. This shifts the design challenge: how does architecture create its own drama on flat land?

Below are precedents organised by design strategy rather than by project. Each strategy offers a different answer to the flat-land question. All retain relevance to the "5 Villas, 5 Statements" framework.

Design Strategies for Flat Sites

Six architectural approaches to creating drama and enclosure on flat terrain

🏗️

Excavation & Sunken Courtyards

Dig down to create sectional drama. The building becomes the landscape — unseen from approach, revealed upon entry. Creates privacy, thermal mass, and a grounded experience.

🌊

Water Mirror / Reflecting Pool

Horizontal water surfaces become the ground plane — extending the bay onto the site. The villa floats on a sheet of reflection. Sky and water merge. Particularly powerful with Hạ Long Bay's karst backdrop.

🏛️

Pavilion Scatter / Horizontal Spread

Instead of one mass, fragment the villa into multiple pavilions across the site. Creates journey, discovery, and multiple view axes. Courtyards between pavilions become outdoor rooms.

🏔️

Artificial Topography

Sculpt the ground — berms, mounds, terraced earth. The site becomes a sculpted landscape. Buildings sit within, on top of, or at the base of created landforms.

🎬

Long Axis / Procession Approach

A controlled approach sequence — long walkway, water channel, or walled garden — builds anticipation before the bay reveal. The arrival IS the experience.

🖼️

Elevated / Flying Volume

Lift the main living volume above the flat ground plane. The view is amplified by height. Below becomes shaded outdoor space. The form becomes a visible marker.

Reference Projects by Strategy

Each project demonstrates a design approach applicable to a flat site with water views

⛰️ Excavation & Sunken Courtyard

🏠 Sunken
Earth-sheltered Flat site

Villa Vals

SeARCH + CMA · Vals, Switzerland
A house completely concealed within a hillside — excavated into the slope with only a curved stone wall visible at ground level. Reached through a tunnel from a nearby barn. Protected by strict landscape preservation law, the architects buried the entire program. A dramatic oval opening brings light and views into the sunken courtyard.
→ Flat site application: Excavate the villa into the flat ground. The roof becomes a green meadow, invisible from approach. A sunken courtyard at the centre provides privacy and framed views toward the bay. The "disappearing villa" — revealed only upon descent.

🌿 Sunken
Excavated Flat site

Sunken House — Adjaye Associates

Adjaye Associates · London, UK
An infill house standing in an excavated courtyard — not overlooked by adjacent buildings. The main living space sits at this lower level, with privacy created entirely by excavation. The surrounding garden at ground level becomes a secret green buffer.
→ Flat site application: At Lam Ngọc, excavate the villa programme by 1.5-3 metres. Create a private realm at the lower level while the "roof" (at original grade) is a lush garden with open views to the bay. Privacy from neighbours without walls.

🌊 Water Mirror / Reflecting Pool

🏛️ Water as ground
Reflecting pool Flat site icon

Amanzoe

Edward Tuttle / Designrealization · Porto Heli, Greece
A modern Acropolis on a hilltop — colonnades, pavilions, and long reflecting pools frame panoramic views of the Aegean. The architecture is classically Aman: strong horizontal lines, pale stone, symmetrical water features that mirror the sky. Long pools extend from pavilions toward the sea horizon, visually merging architecture with ocean.
→ Flat site application: A long reflecting pool — 20-40m — extending from the villa's main pavilion toward the bay. The water surface captures Hạ Long Bay's karsts in reflection. At night, illuminated water creates a second sky. The building appears to float between two bodies of water.

🔲 Water mirror
Snøhetta Water integration

Svart — Snøhetta

Snøhetta · Svartisen, Norway
World's first self-powered hotel. A perfect circular form on stilts over an Arctic fjord. The ring shape provides panoramic views. The building touches the water lightly — on stilts, not foundations. Energy-positive (85% less energy). The form was inspired by local fish-drying racks.
→ Flat site application: While Svart is over water, its lesson for Lam Ngọc is: a waterfront villa on a flat site can be lifted on slender columns above a reflecting pool. The water continues under the building. The villa floats. The circular/curved form becomes an instantly recognisable silhouette.

🏛️ Pavilion Scatter / Horizontal Spread

🌴 Pavilions
WOHA Camouflaged

Alila Villas Uluwatu

WOHA · Bali, Indonesia
A series of semi-transparent rectangular boxes perched on the edge of Bali's limestone cliffs. The design blends contemporary clean lines with traditional Balinese materials — local stone, recycled railway sleepers, open pavilions. "Camouflaged architecture" — buildings overtaken by landscaping. Green roofs. Indoor-outdoor boundaries dissolved.
→ Flat site application: On flat land, WOHA's pavilion-scatter approach becomes even more relevant. Each villa as a cluster of pavilions — sleeping, living, dining, bathing — connected by garden paths. Courtyards between pavilions become outdoor rooms. Green roofs make the architecture disappear when viewed from above.

🏡 Pavilions
Kengo Kuma Negative arch.

Red Sea Villas — Kengo Kuma

Kengo Kuma · Saudi Arabia
7 villa typologies on a pristine island. Kuma's design approach was "informed by the rich variety of coral life covering the surrounding ocean floor." Overwater and land-based villas that reference marine forms. Designed to be low-impact on the fragile coastal ecosystem.
→ Flat site application: Each villa type is different — designed for a specific site condition. On flat land, the differentiation comes from: orientation to bay, relationship to water (on water / on land / half-submerged), and roof form. Kuma's "negative architecture" — where the building yields to the landscape — is the right philosophy for a flat site where the VIEW is the asset.

🏔️ Artificial Topography

🪨 Carved into rock
Mountain integration Lantern effect

Desert Rock — Oppenheim Architecture

Oppenheim Architecture · Hejaz Mountains, Saudi Arabia
Resort carved into granite mountains — cave suites integrated into natural rock outcrops. Buildings "camouflaged during the day, glowing like lanterns at night." Architecture forms found in cooler microclimates. Buildings seem to "detach from rock." Night lighting transforms structures into "small lanterns dotted across the massif."
→ Flat site application: On a flat site, the "lantern effect" translates to: each villa as a sculpted earth form — green mounds with glowing apertures at night. Artificial hills built above ground (berms) conceal the rooms below. At night, 5 lit forms across the site = 5 stars.

Costa Rica Hillside — Green Roof Villa

Undisclosed · Costa Rica
A luxury home that "blends with its wild environment thanks to a low profile and a green roof." Unassuming industrial facade encasing a serenely luxurious interior. The building is practically invisible from above — green roof merges with surrounding jungle.

🎬 Long Axis / Procession Approach

🏗️ Procession
Tadao Ando Concrete axis

Casa Wabi — Tadao Ando

Tadao Ando · Puerto Escondido, Mexico
A 312-metre-long concrete wall provides the framework for this artist retreat on the Mexican coast. The wall separates public from private, frames the view, and creates a powerful axial approach. Six small guest cabins sit to the west, each with a palapa roof and ocean view. The wall is both a gesture and a spine — organising space across a flat coastal site.
→ Flat site application: A signature wall — long, horizontal, precise — as the organising device for one of the villas. The wall extends from the building toward the bay, framing the view. Guests walk along it, turn the corner, and the entire bay is revealed. A single gesture gives the flat site its drama.

🔲 Journey
Kengo Kuma Water Cherry

Water Cherry Villa — Kengo Kuma

Kengo Kuma · Atami, Japan
A seaside villa that abstracts traditional Japanese form. Architecture "disappears" — yielding to cherry blossoms and the sea view. Extensive natural materials: wood, stone, glass. Negative architecture — the building frames rather than claims the landscape.
→ Flat site application: For a flat site, Kuma's approach is: let the building be a sequence of framed views. A path that turns, a water feature that catches the light, a pavilion that opens to the bay. The villa is experienced as a journey — not a single object but a series of discoveries.

🖼️ Elevated / Flying Volume

🏗️ Cantilevered
BIG Setouchi

NOT A HOTEL Setouchi — BIG

BIG (Bjarke Ingels Group) · Sagishima, Japan
Three villas on a 30,000m² cape, each with a different orientation (180°/270°/360°). Rammed earth construction using local materials. Japanese + Scandinavian fusion. Villas follow the site's natural contours. Each villa designed as one large space with skylit pods for bathrooms and storage. Traditional Japanese baths, infinity pools.
→ Flat site application: BIG's approach is to orient each villa uniquely. On a flat site at Lam Ngọc, the differentiation comes from: viewing axis (each villa aimed at a different karst), height (one elevated on columns, one at ground level, one partially submerged), and roof form (each with a distinct silhouette). The flat site forces design creativity — you can't rely on topography, only on architecture.

Boat-only Arrival

NOT A HOTEL Rusutsu — Snøhetta

Snøhetta · Hokkaidō, Japan
One mountaintop ski villa. Two gently curving linear volumes positioned perpendicularly, each dipping at the centre in homage to the surrounding mountains. The building's form is derived from the landscape, not imposed on it. A single Snøhetta-designed villa elevates an entire project.
→ Flat site application: A single elevated volume on slender columns — lifted just enough to catch the breeze and the view. Below: shaded outdoor living. Above: a floating glass pavilion with unimpeded 270° view. The lifting creates the drama that topography would otherwise provide.

5 Villas for Flat Terrain

Revised typologies — each uses a different flat-site strategy

♢ Revised "5 Villas, 5 Strategies"

01

The Sunken Sanctuary

Excavated courtyard. Invisible from approach. Roof = green meadow. Only the view betrays its presence. Strategy: Excavation.

02

The Floating Pavilion

Elevated on slender columns above a reflecting pool. Glass walls dissolve the boundary. Bay appears to enter the room. Strategy: Elevated + Water Mirror.

03

The Scattered Courtyards

Five pavilions connected by garden paths. Each pavilion frames a different view of a single karst. Strategy: Horizontal spread.

04

The Lantern Mound

Artificial hill with rooms carved inside. At night, light spills from a single aperture — like a lantern on the shoreline. Strategy: Artificial topography.

05

The Bridge House

A single long volume spanning between two artificial ponds. Arrival via boat to a dock beneath the span. The building IS a gateway. Strategy: Procession + Axis.

Design Notes for Flat Sites

Observations distilled from all case studies

① Flat ≠ Boring

A flat site is a blank canvas. The most memorable projects in the world (Amanzoe, Casa Wabi, Amangiri) are on flat or gently sloping land. They create their own topography through architecture, water, and landscape design.

② The View is the Only Given

Without natural features to build around, the view to Hạ Long Bay becomes the single unifying orientation. Every villa must face the bay differently — not just rotated by degrees, but a fundamentally different relationship to the waterline.

③ Water is the New Topography

On a flat site, water — reflecting pools, canals, plunge pools, water gardens — becomes the primary landscape material. It creates a new ground plane that mirrors the sky and the karsts beyond.

④ Silhouette Matters More

With no cliffs or hills to frame it, each villa's silhouette must work against the Hạ Long Bay skyline. A flat site means the building's profile is visible from the bay. The 5 silhouettes become the project's identity.

⑤ Arrival by Water Becomes the Drama

The flat site offers no dramatic approach — except the water arrival. This elevates the boat journey (Tuan Chau → villa dock) to the most important architectural experience of the project. The channel, the dock, the reveal.

⑥ Night Lighting = The New Entry

From the bay at night, the 5 villas will be seen as 5 lit signatures before the architecture is legible. The night lighting — lantern, glow, reflection — is the first impression for any arriving guest.
