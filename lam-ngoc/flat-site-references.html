Design precedents for a flat site with views to Hạ Long Bay — architecture that creates its own landscape

5 Villas · Flat Terrain · No Existing Topography

♢ New Site Context: Flat Terrain

The Lam Ngọc site is confirmed flat — no existing cliffs, hills, or rock formations to build around. The primary natural asset is the view to Hạ Long Bay's limestone karsts. This shifts the design challenge: how does architecture create its own drama on flat land?

Below are precedents mapped directly to the 5 villa strategies. Each strategy offers a different design answer to the flat-land question. Case studies verify the thinking has been tested at the highest level globally.

01

V1 — The Sunken Sanctuary · Excavation & Sunken Courtyard

Dig down to create sectional drama. The building becomes the landscape — unseen from approach, revealed upon entry. Creates privacy, thermal mass, and a grounded experience. Green roof merges with the flat terrain.

🏠Sunken
Earth-shelteredFlat site

Villa Vals

SeARCH + CMA · Vals, Switzerland
A house completely concealed within a hillside — excavated into the slope with only a curved stone wall visible at ground level. Reached through a tunnel from a nearby barn. Protected by strict landscape preservation law, the architects buried the entire program. A dramatic oval opening brings light and views into the sunken courtyard.
→ Lam Ngọc application: Excavate the villa into the flat ground. The roof becomes a green meadow, invisible from approach. A sunken courtyard at the centre provides privacy and framed views toward the bay. The "disappearing villa" — revealed only upon descent.

ExcavatedFlat site

Sunken House — Adjaye Associates

Adjaye Associates · London, UK
An infill house standing in an excavated courtyard. The main living space sits at a lower level, with privacy created entirely by excavation. The surrounding garden at ground level becomes a secret green buffer. A three-storey house in a conservation area — the site was excavated to basement level creating a sunken patio.
→ Lam Ngọc application: Excavate the villa programme by 1.5-3 metres. Create a private realm at the lower level while the roof (at original grade) is a lush garden with open views to the bay. Privacy from neighbours without walls.

02

V2 — The Floating Pavilion · Elevated Volume + Water Mirror

Lift the main living volume above the flat ground plane. Below becomes a reflecting pool that merges sky, water, and the bay beyond. The building appears to float. View amplified by height. The silhouette becomes a visible marker.

🏛️Water mirror
Reflecting poolEdward Tuttle

Amanzoe

Edward Tuttle / Designrealization · Porto Heli, Greece
A modern Acropolis on a hilltop — colonnades, pavilions, and long reflecting pools frame panoramic views of the Aegean. Strong horizontal lines, pale stone, symmetrical water features that mirror the sky. Long pools extend from pavilions toward the sea horizon, visually merging architecture with ocean.
→ Lam Ngọc application: A long reflecting pool — 20-40m — extending from the villa's main pavilion toward the bay. The water surface captures Hạ Long Bay's karsts in reflection. At night, illuminated water creates a second sky.

🏗️Cantilevered
BIGSetouchi

NOT A HOTEL Setouchi — BIG

BIG (Bjarke Ingels Group) · Sagishima, Japan
Three villas on a 30,000m² cape, each with a different orientation (180°/270°/360°). Rammed earth construction using local materials. Japanese + Scandinavian fusion. Villas follow the site's natural contours. Each villa designed as one large space with skylit pods. Traditional Japanese baths, infinity pools.
→ Lam Ngọc application: On a flat site, differentiation comes from viewing axis (each villa aimed at a different karst), height (one elevated, one at ground, one submerged), and roof form (each with a distinct silhouette). The flat site forces design creativity.

🔲Floating form
SnøhettaWater integration

Svart — Snøhetta

Snøhetta · Svartisen, Norway
World's first self-powered hotel. A perfect circular form on stilts over an Arctic fjord. The ring shape provides panoramic views. The building touches the water lightly — on stilts, not foundations. Form inspired by local fish-drying racks. 85% less energy than equivalent hotel.
→ Lam Ngọc application: A waterfront villa on flat site can be lifted on slender columns above a reflecting pool. The water continues under the building. The villa floats. The circular/curved form becomes an instantly recognisable silhouette against the bay.

03

V3 — The Scattered Courtyards · Pavilion Scatter / Horizontal Spread

Fragment the villa into multiple pavilions across the site. Creates journey, discovery, and multiple view axes. Courtyards between pavilions become outdoor rooms. Green roofs make the architecture disappear when viewed from above.

🌴Pavilions
WOHACamouflaged

Alila Villas Uluwatu — WOHA

WOHA · Bali, Indonesia
A series of semi-transparent rectangular boxes on Bali's limestone cliffs. Contemporary clean lines blended with traditional Balinese materials — local stone, recycled railway sleepers, open pavilions. "Camouflaged architecture" — buildings overtaken by landscaping. Green roofs. Indoor-outdoor boundaries dissolved. Each villa has its own saltwater pool.
→ Lam Ngọc application: Each villa as a cluster of pavilions — sleeping, living, dining, bathing — connected by garden paths. Courtyards between pavilions become outdoor rooms. Green roofs make the architecture disappear when viewed from above or from the bay.

Red Sea Villas — Kengo Kuma

Kengo Kuma · Saudi Arabia
7 villa typologies on a pristine island. Kuma's design approach was "informed by the rich variety of coral life covering the surrounding ocean floor." Overwater and land-based villas that reference marine forms. Low-impact on the fragile coastal ecosystem.
→ Lam Ngọc application: Each villa type is different — designed for a specific site condition. On flat land, differentiation comes from orientation to bay, relationship to water (on water / on land / half-submerged), and roof form. Kuma's "negative architecture" — where the building yields to the landscape — is the right philosophy where the VIEW is the asset.

North Island, Seychelles

Silvio Rech + Lesley Carstens · Seychelles
"Each villa handcrafted from local stone, wood, and glass — in sync with nature's elements. Your footprints are the only evidence on untouched beaches." V4 — The Lantern Mound · Artificial Topography

Sculpt the ground — berms, mounds, terraced earth. The site becomes a sculpted landscape. Buildings sit within or under created landforms. At night, each mound glows as a single light source — like lanterns across the shoreline.

🪨Carved
Mountain integrationLantern effect

Desert Rock — Oppenheim Architecture

Oppenheim Architecture · Hejaz Mountains, Saudi Arabia
"Buildings that disappear within the tectonic landscape, echoing ancient Nabatean civilizations — camouflaged during the day, glowing like lanterns at night." Mountain cave suites integrated into rock outcrops. Cliffside plunge pools within the mountain. Night lighting transforms structures into "small lanterns dotted across the massif."
→ Lam Ngọc application: On flat site, the "lantern effect" translates to each villa as a sculpted earth form — green mounds with glowing apertures at night. Artificial hills built above ground (berms) conceal the rooms below. At night, 5 lit forms across the site = 5 stars. The lighting is the architecture.

Green roofInvisible house

Costa Rica — Green Roof Villa

Designed by WMR Arquitectos · Costa Rica
"Blends with its wild environment thanks to a low profile and a green roof." Unassuming industrial facade encasing a serenely luxurious interior. The building is practically invisible from above — green roof merges with surrounding jungle. Powered by solar energy. Uses locally-sourced timber.
→ Lam Ngọc application: On a flat site, building earth-sheltered (partially below grade + green roof) achieves the disappearing act. From the bay approach, all you see is a grassy mound. Inside, the front wall opens completely to the view — the bay unfurls at eye level.

V5 — The Bridge House · Long Axis / Procession Approach

A controlled approach sequence — long walkway, water channel, or walled garden — builds anticipation before the bay reveal. The arrival becomes the first architectural experience. A single long volume frames the view like a lens.

🏗️Procession
Tadao AndoConcrete axis

Casa Wabi — Tadao Ando

Tadao Ando · Puerto Escondido, Mexico
A 312-metre-long concrete wall provides the framework for this artist retreat on the Mexican coast. The wall separates public from private, frames the view, and creates a powerful axial approach. Six small guest cabins sit to the west, each with a palapa roof and ocean view. The wall is both a gesture and a spine — organising space across a flat coastal site.
→ Lam Ngọc application: A signature wall — long, horizontal, precise — as the organising device for one villa. The wall extends from the building toward the bay, framing the view. Guests walk along it, turn the corner, and the entire bay is revealed. A single gesture gives the flat site its drama.

Water Cherry Villa — Kengo Kuma

Kengo Kuma · Atami, Japan
A seaside villa that abstracts traditional Japanese form. Architecture "disappears" — yielding to cherry blossoms and the sea view. Extensive natural materials: wood, stone, glass. Negative architecture — the building frames rather than claims the landscape. The villa is experienced as a journey: a path that turns, a water feature that catches the light, a pavilion that opens to the bay.
→ Lam Ngọc application: For a flat site, Kuma's approach is to make the building a sequence of framed views. Not a single object but a series of discoveries. The bridge house extends across water — guests walk over the pond to reach the living pavilion, the bay revealed gradually.

NOT A HOTEL Rusutsu — Snøhetta

Snøhetta · Rusutsu, Hokkaidō, Japan
A single 1,200m² mountaintop ski villa. Two gently curving linear volumes positioned perpendicularly, each dipping at the centre in homage to the surrounding mountains. Design Notes for Flat Sites

Observations distilled from all case studies

① Flat ≠ Boring

A flat site is a blank canvas. The most memorable projects in the world — Amanzoe, Casa Wabi, Amangiri — are on flat or gently sloping land. They create their own topography through architecture, water, and landscape design.

② The View is the Only Given

Without natural features to build around, the view to Hạ Long Bay becomes the single unifying orientation. Every villa must face the bay differently — not just rotated, but a fundamentally different relationship to the waterline.

③ Water is the New Topography

On a flat site, water — reflecting pools, canals, plunge pools, water gardens — becomes the primary landscape material. It creates a new ground plane that mirrors the sky and the karsts beyond.

④ Silhouette Matters More

With no cliffs or hills to frame it, each villa's silhouette must work against the Hạ Long Bay skyline. A flat site means the building's profile is visible from the bay. The 5 silhouettes become the project's identity.

⑤ Arrival by Water Becomes the Drama

The flat site offers no dramatic approach — except the water arrival. This elevates the boat journey (Tuan Chau → villa dock) to the most important architectural experience of the project. The channel, the dock, the reveal.

⑥ Night Lighting = The New Entry

From the bay at night, the 5 villas will be seen as 5 lit signatures before the architecture is legible. The night lighting — lantern, glow, reflection — is the first impression for any arriving guest.
